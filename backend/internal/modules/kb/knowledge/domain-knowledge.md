## Domain knowledge: How campus placement usually works
Campus placement normally follows a few steps. The company shares an eligibility rule, such as a minimum CGPA and no current backlogs, and eligible students register. Then comes an online test with aptitude, logical reasoning, verbal and sometimes coding questions. Shortlisted students attend technical interviews, followed by an HR round. Some companies add a group discussion or an assignment. Results are announced and offers are released with joining dates. Preparing for each step separately, and starting well before the drives begin, gives the best chance.

## Domain knowledge: Service companies and product companies
IT service companies build and maintain software for other businesses, hire in large numbers on campus and usually start with training. Product companies build their own software or platforms, hire fewer people and test problem solving and design more deeply. Neither is better for everyone. Service roles can give broad exposure and a stable start; product roles often expect stronger programming and project work. Many people move between the two. Apply to both kinds and prepare the common core: data structures, SQL, communication and projects.

## Domain knowledge: CGPA cut-offs and backlogs
Many companies set a minimum CGPA and ask that students have no pending backlogs, because it is an easy first filter when thousands apply. The exact rule differs by company and is stated in each drive's eligibility. Clearing backlogs early matters more than almost anything else for eligibility. A modest CGPA can be balanced with strong projects, coding practice and good communication, but it cannot fix a failed eligibility rule, so check the rule before investing effort in a drive.

## Domain knowledge: Internships
An internship gives real work experience, a stronger resume and sometimes a path to a job offer. Look for internships through the college placement cell, company career pages, professional networks and faculty contacts. Even a short internship or a well-documented open-source or project contribution helps. During an internship, ask for feedback, keep notes on what you built and get a certificate or letter at the end. Be careful with any offer that asks you to pay a fee for an internship.

## Domain knowledge: Certifications worth considering
A certification helps most when it matches the role you want and when you can discuss what you learned. Reasonable starting points are a cloud fundamentals certificate, a database or SQL course, a programming language certificate from a recognised provider and NPTEL courses with a proctored exam. Do not collect many certificates without projects; one certificate with a project built from it is worth more than five without. Free options are available, and the course catalogue in this system lists some.

## Domain knowledge: Preparing in three months
A simple plan for the last three months before placements. Month one: finish aptitude fundamentals, revise core subjects and start daily coding practice. Month two: continue coding by topic, build or polish two projects, learn SQL and prepare your resume. Month three: take full mock tests under time limits, practise interviews with a friend or a senior, prepare answers for common HR questions and review your weak areas every week. Track your progress weekly and adjust. Consistent daily work beats last-minute effort.

## Domain knowledge: Technical interview preparation
In a technical interview, the way you think matters as much as the answer. For coding questions, restate the problem, ask about edge cases, describe your approach before writing code, write clean code, test it on an example and state its cost. If you are stuck, explain what you tried and think aloud. For your projects, be ready to explain why you chose each technology, what was hard and what you would improve. Review the fundamentals of every subject listed on your resume.

## Domain knowledge: HR interview preparation
HR rounds check communication, attitude and fit. Prepare a clear introduction of about a minute covering your education, skills, a project and what you are looking for. Prepare honest answers to common questions: strengths and weaknesses, why this company, a time you worked in a team, a time you failed and what you learned, and where you see yourself in a few years. Research the company, be on time, dress neatly and ask one or two thoughtful questions at the end. Never exaggerate or claim skills you do not have.

## Domain knowledge: Core engineering careers for ECE, EEE and mechanical students
Core roles use the fundamentals of your own branch: electronics and embedded design, power systems and control, manufacturing, design and testing. Many students also enter IT roles, which is a valid choice if programming interests you. To keep core options open, keep subject marks strong, learn the tools used in your field (for example CAD, simulation or PCB design), do a core project or internship and follow the recruiters who visit your branch. If you want both, build programming skills alongside your core subjects rather than abandoning one.

## Domain knowledge: Higher studies versus placement
Higher studies suit students who want research, specialised roles or a change of direction. Common routes include GATE for postgraduate admission in India, an MS abroad, or an MBA. Placement suits students who want to start working and learn on the job. The choice depends on your goals, finances and interests, not on what others do. Some students do both by taking a job first and studying later. Speak to faculty and alumni and look at how the entrance exams work before deciding.

## Domain knowledge: Offers, CTC and joining
An offer letter lists the cost to company, which is not the same as monthly take-home pay: it can include benefits, bonuses and deductions. Read the joining date, work location, training or bond terms, notice period and any conditions before accepting. Keep your documents ready for verification. Do not pay anyone to get a job, and confirm that an offer is genuine by checking with the placement cell. If you hold more than one offer, follow your college's placement policy.

## Domain knowledge: Building projects that impress
A good student project solves a real problem for real users, even a small one, such as a college timetable tool. It uses a database, has a clean user interface or API, includes tests, is deployed or easy to run, and has a README that explains the purpose, the technologies and how to start it. Show what you decided and why: employers care about your thinking. Finish fewer projects well rather than starting many. Be ready to explain every part of your own project.

## Domain knowledge: Using AI tools responsibly while learning
AI tools can explain concepts, suggest ideas and help find mistakes, but they can also be wrong and they can stop you from learning if you copy answers. Use them to understand, then close the tool and try to rewrite the solution yourself. Check facts against course material and documentation. Never submit generated work as your own where it is not allowed, and do not paste private data into tools you do not control. In interviews you will be asked to explain and write code yourself.
